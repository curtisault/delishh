## Equipment

- Heavy 25 cm skillet, cast iron, wide — the evaporation surface is part of the recipe
- Whisk
- Fine strainer, within reach — see Rescues

## Ingredients

### The roux — equal parts, by volume

- 4 tbsp sausage or bacon drippings, butter if you have none
- 4 tbsp all-purpose flour

### The milk — whole, no substitutions

- 2 cups whole milk, warmed
- 2 tbsp heavy cream, off the heat at the end

### The seasoning — last, always

- 1 tsp black pepper, coarsely cracked — plus more, then more
- ½ tsp kosher salt
- cayenne, a pinch, optional

### For sawmill — the version worth making

- 450 g breakfast sausage, bulk — its drippings replace the fat above

## Steps

1. For sawmill gravy, brown the sausage in the dry skillet, break it
   fine, then lift the meat out and leave every drop of fat and every
   brown speck behind. Otherwise melt the drippings over medium heat.
   The fond is the flavor — butter alone makes a competent gravy;
   pork fat makes the one you are actually thinking of.
   cue: 8 MIN · UNTIL THE FAT SHIMMERS

2. Whisk the flour in all at once and keep it moving. Blond, not
   brown — browning the roux cuts its thickening power and turns
   white gravy beige. Pull the pan off the burner the moment it wants
   to take on color.
   cue: 90 S · SMELLS LIKE TOAST, LOOKS LIKE WET SAND

3. Pour in about half a cup of the milk and whisk hard. Lumps are
   decided here: a tight paste has nowhere to hide flour, a loose one
   traps it in beads you cannot whisk out later.
   cue: GLOSSY PASTE, NO DRY SPOTS AT THE EDGE

4. Add the remaining milk in two or three pours, whisking smooth
   between each. Scrape the corners of the pan where the whisk does
   not reach. Warm milk goes in without seizing the roux.
   cue: 2 MIN · SMOOTH BETWEEN POURS

5. Bring to a bare simmer — lazy bubbles, not a boil — and cook,
   whisking often. This is the step everyone skips, and it is the
   difference between gravy and paste: flour needs time and heat to
   lose its raw taste, and no amount of seasoning covers it.
   cue: 8 MIN · COATS A SPOON, HOLDS A LINE

6. Kill the burner. Stir in the cream, then the pepper, salt, and
   cayenne. Taste. Add pepper past the point that feels reasonable,
   then check the salt again — the drippings are already salted and
   you cannot take it back out.
   cue: OFF THE HEAT · SEASON LAST

7. Fold the sausage back in for sawmill. Loosen with milk a splash at
   a time, or simmer another minute to tighten. Serve at once over
   split biscuits — it thickens as it cools and again on the plate.
   cue: PULL IT LOOSER THAN YOU WANT TO EAT IT

## Watchpoints

- Blond roux, never browned. Color costs thickening power.
- A bare simmer only, and never a hard boil after the cream goes in — that is how it breaks.
- Whole milk is the recipe. Skim or 2% makes a thin, chalky gravy that tastes like the flour never left; if 2% is all there is, swap a quarter cup of it for heavy cream.
- Coarse-cracked pepper, not pre-ground dust. Dust disappears into the milk and reads as dull heat; flecks are what this is supposed to taste like.

## Rescues

- **Lumps** — Too much milk at once at the paste stage. Push it through a strainer now; tighten the first pour next time.
- **Tastes like paste** — The simmer got cut short. Back on low for another five minutes. Fully recoverable.
- **Thin, and stays thin** — Skim milk, or not enough simmer. Never stir in more flour; simmer it down instead.
- **Broken or grainy** — A hard boil after the cream went in. Whisk in a splash of cold milk off the heat; it usually comes back.
- **Flat** — Underseasoned, and it is almost always the pepper. It should have real bite, not a hint.

## Keeps

- Fridge, 3 days. It sets solid; loosen over low heat with milk a splash at a time.
- Holds at 95 °C / 200 °F, lid on, up to 30 minutes.
- Do not freeze — the starch and the milk fat come apart, and no amount of whisking puts them back.
- Never microwave it at full power. It separates in about forty seconds.

## Note

The ratio is the whole card: fat, flour, milk at 4 tbsp : 4 tbsp :
2 cups for the thick version that stands up on a biscuit — drop the
fat and flour to 3 for chicken-fried steak, 2 for creamed dishes,
milk never moves. Drippings first, bacon fat second, butter third;
bacon brings smoke that some tables love and some do not want
anywhere near a biscuit. Serve it over split buttermilk biscuits,
chicken-fried steak, fried pork chops, or a pile of hash browns with
a runny egg on top — anything that needs a salty, peppery blanket.
