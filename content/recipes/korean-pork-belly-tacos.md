## Equipment

- Immersion circulator and a container it clamps to — eight hours at one temperature is the method, not a shortcut to it
- Vacuum sealer and bags, or a zip-top bag and a sink of water to press the air out
- Cast iron skillet wide enough that the chunks stand apart — crowded belly steams
- Wire rack over a sheet pan, for the overnight chill

## Ingredients

### The belly — start it two days before

- 900 g skin-on pork belly
- 2 tbsp soy sauce
- 4 cloves garlic
- 1 tbsp fresh ginger, sliced

### The sweet glaze

- 2 tbsp hoisin sauce
- 1 tbsp doenjang or white miso
- 2 tbsp brown sugar
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 1 tbsp sesame oil

### The sesame slaw — dress it last

- 225 g Napa cabbage, shredded
- 1 carrot, julienned
- 1 tbsp honey
- 1 tbsp rice vinegar
- 1 tbsp sesame oil

### To serve

- 12 small flour tortillas
- ¼ cup Kewpie mayonnaise
- 3 scallions, thinly sliced
- 1 tbsp sesame seeds, toasted
- 2 tbsp avocado oil, for the sear

## Steps

1. Seal the belly with the soy sauce, garlic and ginger, pressing
   the bag flat so the slab sits in one layer. A slab folded on
   itself cooks to two different textures and only one of them is
   the one you want.
   cue: ONE FLAT LAYER · NO AIR AGAINST THE SKIN

2. Cook it at 77 °C / 170 °F. This is the hold that renders the fat
   and leaves the meat sliceable — hotter pulls it to the falling
   apart stage, which is excellent barbecue and cannot be cut into
   a chunk that holds an edge in a pan.
   cue: 8–10 H · TENDER, STILL HOLDING ITS SHAPE

3. Drop the sealed bag into ice water, then refrigerate overnight on
   a rack, bag opened and the slab drained. Two things happen here,
   and both are the recipe: the belly firms up enough to cut clean,
   and the skin dries out. Searing a warm, damp slab is the one
   mistake that cannot be recovered later.
   cue: 20 MIN ICE · THEN COLD AND DRY OVERNIGHT

4. Cut into 4 cm chunks and pat every face dry, the skin twice. Any
   water left on the surface boils before the fat can render, and
   what you get is a grey, leathery skin that never crisps.
   cue: BONE DRY ON EVERY FACE

5. Whisk the hoisin, doenjang, brown sugar, mirin and sesame oil
   smooth. It should taste sweet first, then salty, with the miso
   underneath it. Thin it with a spoon of water if it will not fall
   off the whisk.
   cue: SWEET FIRST, SALT SECOND, FALLS OFF THE WHISK

6. Dress the cabbage and carrot with the honey, vinegar and sesame
   oil, fold in half the scallions, and leave it ten minutes. Longer
   and the honey draws the water out — the slaw goes limp and the
   tortilla underneath it goes wet.
   cue: 10 MIN · WILTED AT THE EDGES, STILL CRISP

7. Heat the avocado oil in the cast iron until it is properly
   smoking. Lay the chunks skin down and leave them alone, then turn
   through the other faces. Avocado oil, not sesame — sesame oil is
   a finishing oil and turns acrid well under this heat.
   cue: 3–4 MIN SKIN DOWN · BLISTERED AND GOLDEN

8. Pour in half the glaze for the last sixty seconds and turn the
   chunks through it as it bubbles and grips. Sixty seconds, on a
   timer. It is sugar in a smoking pan, and it goes from lacquer to
   carbon inside the next minute.
   cue: 60 SEC · GLOSSY AND CLINGING, NOT SMOKING

9. Warm the tortillas dry, in the same pan or straight over a
   flame. Spread each with Kewpie, pile on slaw, then the pork,
   drizzle the rest of the glaze, and finish with the last of the
   scallions and the sesame seeds.
   cue: MAYO, SLAW, PORK — IN THAT ORDER

## Watchpoints

- 77 °C / 170 °F is the whole argument for this method. It renders the fat over eight hours and stops short of shredding, which is what lets a chunk take a hard sear and still be a chunk.
- Cold and dry before it meets the pan. Chilled overnight uncovered is not a convenience step — it is what makes the crust possible.
- Avocado oil for the sear. Sesame oil smokes and turns bitter far below the heat this needs, and it will take the glaze down with it.
- The glaze goes in at the end, never at the start. Sugar over a smoking skillet has about ninety seconds in it.
- Dress the slaw ten minutes out, not before. It is the water in a wilted slaw that ruins the tortilla, not the slaw.

## Rescues

- **Skin leathery and grey, never crisped** — It went into the pan damp, or straight from a warm bag. Put the chunks back on a rack in the fridge uncovered for a few hours and sear again; the surface has to be dry before it can brown.
- **Glaze black, and bitter all the way through** [terminal] — It went in more than a minute or two before the pork came out. Burnt sugar does not come back, and it has coated everything. Wipe the pan, sear the next batch clean, and glaze off the heat.
- **Belly shredding on the board instead of cutting** — The bath ran hot. Serve it as is, piled rather than cubed; it is very good and it is not this recipe. Check the circulator against a second thermometer before the next one.
- **Pale after four minutes skin down** — The pan was not hot enough when the pork went in, or the chunks are touching. Take them out, get the pan back to smoking, and work in two batches.
- **Slaw sitting in a pool** — Dressed too early. Lift the cabbage out with a slotted spoon and leave the liquid behind; do not wring it, which takes the dressing with it.

## Keeps

- The sealed bag out of the ice bath is the thing that waits. Fridge, unopened, and sear the day you are eating — this method is built around cooking it ahead.
- Sear only what is going on the table. Belly that has been seared, chilled and seared again is tough, and no amount of glaze covers it.
- The glaze keeps in a jar in the fridge and is worth doubling. It will thicken cold; loosen it with a spoon of water over low heat.
- The slaw is made to order. It weeps inside the hour and there is no reviving it.
- Reheat seared pork on a rack in a hot oven, uncovered, until the fat runs again. A microwave turns the skin to rubber in thirty seconds.

## Note

Everything here is built around one idea: the pork has to be cold,
firm and dry before it ever sees the pan. The circulator gets it
tender without taking it past the point where a chunk holds an edge,
and the night uncovered in the fridge dries the skin out. Skip either
half and the sear is a gamble — some pieces crisp, the rest slump and
steam. That is the whole reason this is a two-day recipe for fifty
minutes of actual work, and nearly all of that work is on the second
day.

The kimchi is missing on purpose. Honey, rice vinegar and sesame on
raw cabbage does the same structural job — something sharp and cold
landing against hot fat — while leaving the glaze as the loudest
thing on the taco, which is how I want it here. Doenjang if there is
any in the fridge, white miso if not; the miso is milder and a shade
sweeter, and it suits the sweetness rather than fighting it.
